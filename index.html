Abstractچکیده

Background. Intelligent systems increasingly act on a changing model of a person, but the literature that builds such models is split across dynamic user modeling (DUM), recommender systems (RecSys) and intelligent matching (IM), and the three rarely share a definition of what is being represented.

Objective. To synthesise ten benchmark studies published 2021–2026 through a seven-dimensional account of human state representation (D1–D7), and to derive a taxonomy of the research gaps the corpus leaves open.

Methods. A protocol-driven structured review: five Boolean strings across four databases; eligibility criteria fixed before screening; a six-field extraction schema with four observable appraisal attributes; comparative and thematic synthesis rather than meta-analysis; a purposive benchmark corpus of ten studies (seven empirical, three reviews/surveys); reporting mapped to a PRISMA 2020-informed checklist, with deviations stated.

Results. Two dimensions dominate: history (D2) and drift (D7) are modelled by nine of the ten studies, while constraints (D5) and environment (D6) appear in three and two respectively, and the median study covers four of the seven dimensions. One study spans all seven, but through separate components rather than a single joint objective. Three strands explain the pattern — temporal sequence models, LLM-based agents, and bilateral/lifelong adaptation — each expanding one dimension at the cost of another.

Conclusions. Six gaps follow; the two Tier A gaps, a joint seven-dimensional objective and streaming cold-start scoring, are prerequisites for the other four. The review's own limitations — a purposive corpus, unarchived screening counts, and single-coder extraction — are reported rather than hidden.

01/ 11

Introduction

مقدمه

Intelligent systems increasingly act on a model of a person rather than on a single request. A recommender ranks items from an inferred taste, an agent plans a multi-step task from a stated goal, a matching platform pairs two sides of a market from reciprocal preferences. In each case the system must maintain an internal representation of a human state: who the person is, what they have done, what they want now, what they are allowed to want, where they are, and how all of this changes over time.

That representation is studied in at least three communities that rarely share a vocabulary. Dynamic User Modeling (DUM) infers and maintains evolving state vectors or profiles. Recommender Systems (RecSys) filter and rank catalog items for a target user. Intelligent Matching (IM) optimises bilateral satisfaction between two sides of a market under capacity and policy constraints. Reviews of adjacent literatures have mapped time-aware recommendation (Alabduljabbar et al., 2023) and the broader digital-twin agenda (Lin et al., 2024), but no comparative account asks how these three lines operationalise the same human state.

The comparison matters because the dimensions that are hardest to represent are also the least represented. A system that tracks interest but not constraints can recommend the impossible; one that models the environment but not drift will be confidently out of date. Fragmented reporting makes it difficult to see which of these weaknesses is a limitation of one study and which is shared across a field.

This review therefore asks how ten benchmark studies from 2021–2026 operationalise a seven-dimensional human state (D1–D7), which architectural mechanisms they propose for intent, drift and constraints, which dimensions they systematically under-serve, and which structural research gaps follow from that coverage pattern.

Contributions of this review

  1. A seven-dimensional operationalisation (D1–D7) that makes “dynamic human representation” comparable across DUM, RecSys and IM instead of treating the three literatures separately.
  2. A six-field comparative matrix over ten benchmark studies, plus a dimension-coverage map derived from the same extraction, so that gaps in reporting are visible rather than inferred.
  3. A critical synthesis of three research strands — micro-temporal sequences, LLM-driven profiling and agents, and bilateral/lifelong adaptation — and of the trade-offs each strand accepts.
  4. A six-item gap taxonomy (G1–G6) with explicit tiers of urgency and one testable research question per gap.

02/ 11

Research questions and review protocol

سؤال‌های پژوهش و پروتکل مرور

The review answers four questions. RQ1 and RQ2 are descriptive; RQ3 and RQ4 are analytical and produce the taxonomy in §7.

  1. How does the corpus operationalise each of the seven representational state dimensions (D1–D7)?
  2. Which architectural mechanisms are proposed for capturing micro-temporal intent, preference drift, and hard constraints?
  3. Which dimensions are systematically under-represented across the corpus, and with what shared consequences?
  4. Which structural research gaps follow from that coverage pattern, and in what order of urgency should they be addressed?
Review protocol

The protocol — research questions, Boolean strings, eligibility criteria, and the six-field extraction schema — was fixed before extraction began and is reported in §3. The review was not prospectively registered, and the selection was purposive rather than exhaustive (the benchmark corpus is specified by the course assignment); both facts are treated as limitations in §8 rather than as deviations hidden from the reader.

03/ 11

Method

روش

The review follows a structured, protocol-driven design: a fixed search strategy, explicit eligibility criteria, a six-field extraction schema applied uniformly to every study, and a qualitative comparative-plus-thematic synthesis. Reporting is organised against a PRISMA 2020-informed checklist (Appendix A) with the deviations named in §3.5 and §8.

3.1 · Search strategy

To construct a corpus grounded strictly in 2021–2026 peer-reviewed literature, the group executed targeted Boolean queries across Google Scholar, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, and ScienceDirect. Strings were written per target dimension and combined with the review window; the five strings in Table 1 are the queries actually applied, reproduced verbatim.

3.2 · Eligibility criteria

A study entered the corpus only if it satisfied every inclusion criterion and no exclusion criterion. The criteria were written before screening so that they could not be adjusted to fit a preferred paper.

3.3 · Data extraction and appraisal

Every included study was read in full and recorded in a uniform six-field schema. Because the corpus is heterogeneous — surveys, a billion-user deployment, and small-data benchmarks — appraisal is restricted to attributes that can be observed rather than scored: whether the venue is peer-reviewed, whether datasets are disclosed, how many dimensions the study covers, and whether limitations are stated explicitly.

3.4 · Study selection

The strings in Table 1 produced a candidate pool which was screened first on title and abstract and then on full text, against the criteria in Table 2. Ten studies were retained as the benchmark corpus: seven empirical studies and three reviews or surveys, satisfying the assignment's requirement of at least three high-level reviews. The corpus was also balanced across the three literatures, so that no single community supplies more than half of the evidence.

Two facts about this stage are reported as they are, not as the PRISMA flow would ideally show them: the corpus is purposive, in that the assignment specifies a ten-paper benchmark set; and the per-database screening counts were not archived in machine-readable form. Figure 1 therefore records the stages and criteria rather than record counts, and §8 treats this as a review-level limitation.

3.5 · Synthesis approach

Synthesis is comparative and thematic rather than statistical. The ten studies measure different things — NDCG on public benchmarks, bilateral recall, Hit@5 in a deployment, compute cost — on different data, so pooling effect sizes would produce a number with no coherent referent. Instead, the review compares the representational choices the studies make (what they encode, what they assume, what they leave out), groups the mechanisms into three strands (§6), and derives the coverage statistics that ground the gap analysis (§5.3, §7).

Three deviations from the ideal protocol are recorded: the absence of a registered protocol (§2), the purposive corpus and unarchived screening counts (§3.4), and a single-coder assignment in which one member extracted each paper before group audit (§8.1).

04/ 11

Conceptual framework

چارچوب مفهومی

Two things must be separated before the corpus can be compared. First, the three research communities must be distinguished by what they optimise, not by the models they use. Second, “dynamic human representation” must be broken into dimensions that can be observed in a paper's design.

4.1 · Dynamic user modeling, recommender systems, and intelligent matching

A critical prerequisite mandated by instructor feedback is establishing clear conceptual boundaries between DUM, RecSys, and IM, while operationalising the seven core human state dimensions (D1–D7). Table 4 separates the three by research objective, state form, direction of update, and evaluation focus.

4.2 · The seven representational state dimensions

Human state representation in modern intelligent platforms is defined across seven operational dimensions. Each is stated as a question a system must answer, so that a paper can be mapped to it by design rather than by keyword.

D1
Stable personal attributes

Who the person is: demographics, static persona traits, and fixed preference priors.

D2
Historical behavior trajectories

What they have done: sequential clickstreams, historical purchase logs, long-term interaction arrays.

D3
Explicit goals & preferences

What they say they want: stated requirements, explicit ratings, structured profile constraints.

D4
Current intentions & immediate needs

What they want right now: session-specific goals, immediate queries, micro-temporal intents.

D5
Constraints & hard limits

What they are allowed to want: budget limits, geography, mutual capacity rules, governance policies.

D6
Contextual & environmental conditions

Where and when: location, device type, weather, social environment, time of day.

D7
Temporal dynamics & preference drift

How all of it changes: long-term interest shifts, time-decay functions, continuous profile updating.

06/ 11

Thematic synthesis

ترکیب موضوعی

6.1 · Micro-temporal intent & sequential behavior trajectories

۶.۱ — نیت خردزمانی و مسیرهای رفتار دنباله‌ای

The evolution of modern recommender systems has been fundamentally shaped by the transition from static collaborative filtering to micro-temporal sequential behavior modeling. Early neural sequential models captured order dependencies but struggled under severe interaction data sparsity. To overcome this, Xie et al. (2022) introduced CL4SRec, establishing a self-supervised contrastive learning framework that applies data augmentation operators — specifically Item Crop, Item Mask, and Item Reorder — to historical behavior trajectories (D2). By maximizing mutual information between correlated sequence views, CL4SRec demonstrated that neural sequential representations can extract resilient intent vectors (D4) even under sparse log conditions, achieving relative NDCG gains of up to 11.02% across benchmark datasets.

However, pure sequence-based contrastive learning often treats interaction timestamps as ordinal indices rather than continuous temporal signals. Addressing this limitation, Zhang et al. (2022) proposed TARN, which couples explicit feature interaction networks (FIN) with temporal convolutional networks (TCN) to capture fine-grained time-decay functions (D7) alongside contextual environmental conditions (D6). Similarly, Al-Ghossein et al. (2022) synthesized stream-based recommendation architectures (SBRS), demonstrating that real-time user modeling requires explicit concept-drift detection modules to balance historical memory retainment against stale preference purging. Together, these studies confirm that tracking micro-temporal behavior trajectories requires unifying continuous temporal convolution with adaptive memory management.

6.2 · LLM-driven user profiling, agent planning & tool augmentation

۶.۲ — پروفایل‌سازی کاربر با LLM، برنامه‌ریزی عاملی و افزونهٔ ابزار

While sequential neural models excel at pattern recognition over closed item-ID spaces, they are inherently incapable of parsing natural-language constraints (D5) or reasoning over multi-step user goals (D3). As systematically categorized by Tan & Jiang (2023), Large Language Models (LLMs) redefine user modeling by operating across four functional paradigms: Profilers, Enhancers, Controllers, and Evaluators. Despite their linguistic fluidity, standalone LLMs suffer severe catalog hallucination — with Hit@5 dropping to 0.15 on proprietary e-commerce catalogs — due to parametric memory boundaries.

To bridge this architectural gap, Huang et al. (2025) formulated InteRecAgent, pioneering the “brain-and-tools” paradigm. InteRecAgent decouples natural-language intent reasoning from item candidate ranking. By introducing plan-first execution, the LLM controller generates structured tool chains upfront, reducing API latency from N+1 steps to exactly 2 calls per turn. Furthermore, candidate memory bus streaming achieves O(1) prompt token overhead, while dual-profile memory dictionaries maintain persistent long-term taste arrays ({history, like, dislike}). Complementing this, Meng et al. (2025) demonstrated in a billion-user commercial deployment that balancing periodic LoRA fine-tuning with low-cost RAG context ingestion achieves optimal trade-offs between compute latency and dynamic interest adaptation. In cross-domain scenarios where item IDs fail completely, Saadati et al. (2025) showed in RUNSRec that fine-tuned language models encoding textual review embeddings enable seamless zero-shot preference transfer without requiring shared user identifiers.

6.3 · Bilateral matching, continual adaptation & lifelong profile dynamics

۶.۳ — تطبیق دوسویه، انطباق پیوسته و پویایی پروفایل مادام‌العمر

A pervasive blind spot in traditional recommender literature is the assumption of unilateral utility optimization, where systems focus solely on user engagement while ignoring platform or supply-side constraints. Yang et al. (2024) addressed this in CRRS by introducing a causal potential-outcome framework for reciprocal recommendation systems (IM). CRRS models bilateral satisfaction across two-sided matching markets (such as online recruitment and dating), introducing a vacant-slots mechanism to optimize mutual match stability under capacity limits (D5).

Concurrently, maintaining profile fidelity over extended lifelong deployment requires continuous adaptation without catastrophic forgetting. Saadati et al. (2025) addressed distribution shifts in DITTO by introducing forward knowledge transfer (FKT) and backward knowledge transfer (BKT) modules, enabling continual preference tracking across evolving task sequences. Finally, Freitas et al. (2026) provided a narrative synthesis of dynamic user-profiling software, emphasizing that future adaptive intelligent systems must unify explicit user feedback, implicit telemetry, and dynamic belief revision to maintain long-term alignment with evolving human goals.

Read together, the three strands describe one trade-off rather than three independent problems. Each strand expands a different dimension at the cost of another: sequence models gain D2/D7 but assume the item space is static; LLM agents gain D3/D4/D5 but pay in latency and hallucination; bilateral and continual work gains D5 and long-horizon stability but assumes attributes are fixed (CRRS) or task boundaries are known (DITTO). No study in the corpus pays all three costs simultaneously, which is the empirical basis for the first gap in §7.

Research gaps and roadmap

شکاف‌های پژوهشی و نقشهٔ راه

By conducting a rigorous cross-study synthesis of the ten benchmark studies, the group identified six structural research gaps, categorised into tiers A through C by urgency and theoretical impact. Table 8 gives, for each gap, the coverage that exists, the boundary that is not investigated, the bibliographic evidence, and one testable research question.

G1

Unified 7D representation

Tier A · critical
Previous research coverage

Existing systems isolate 1–3 dimensions (e.g. D2/D4 in CL4SRec, D3/D5 in InteRecAgent).

Insufficiently investigated boundary

Jointly optimizing all 7 representational state dimensions within a single unified architecture.

Bibliographic evidence

All 10 benchmark studies (Huang 2025 · Tan 2023 · Saadati 2025)

Context

The one study mapped to all seven dimensions, InteRecAgent, does so with separate components rather than a single joint objective — which is exactly the boundary this gap names.

Proposed research question

How can a single hybrid transformer architecture model explicit goals, temporal drift, and bilateral constraints simultaneously?

G2

Streaming cold-start memory

Tier A · critical
Previous research coverage

InteRecAgent and CL4SRec rely on historical behavioral logs for candidate scoring.

Insufficiently investigated boundary

Scoring new cold-start items lacking behavioral logs within streaming candidate buses.

Bibliographic evidence

Huang et al. (2025, App. C.3) · Saadati et al. (2025, RUNSRec)

Context

Both cited systems assume a log-rich item before it can enter the candidate set, so an unlogged item has no path into the ranking stage.

Proposed research question

How can textual metadata embeddings stream within candidate memory buses without API latency penalties?

G3

Continuous item catalog expansion

Tier B · high
Previous research coverage

DITTO models continual learning over fixed task sequences.

Insufficiently investigated boundary

Scaling FKT/BKT continual transfer to dynamically expanding e-commerce catalogs.

Bibliographic evidence

Saadati et al. (2025, DITTO) · Al-Ghossein et al. (2022)

Context

Task sequences are assumed stable; catalog growth changes the label space itself, which the transfer modules do not model.

Proposed research question

Can continual learning algorithms prevent catastrophic forgetting when item catalog vocabularies double daily?

G4

Bilateral multi-stakeholder governance

Tier B · high
Previous research coverage

CRRS models bilateral reciprocal utility in two-sided matching.

Insufficiently investigated boundary

Harmonizing individual user curiosity with organizational compliance and fairness rules.

Bibliographic evidence

Yang et al. (2024) · Freitas et al. (2026)

Context

Reciprocity is optimized pairwise; governance rules act on the platform as a whole, so the two objectives are never solved in the same objective function.

Proposed research question

How to balance real-time user intent satisfaction against multi-stakeholder governance constraints?

G5

Differentiable prompt–neural spaces

Tier C · medium
Previous research coverage

InteRecAgent and Meng et al. pass discrete JSON profile strings to PyTorch rankers.

Insufficiently investigated boundary

Formulating continuous hybrid neural–symbolic embedding spaces for joint gradient flow.

Bibliographic evidence

Tan & Jiang (2023) · Huang et al. (2025)

Context

The hand-off between the language layer and the ranking layer is a serialization boundary: gradients stop there, so the two halves cannot be trained jointly.

Proposed research question

Can gradient backpropagation flow directly from neural rankers (SASRec) into LLM soft-prompt adapters?

G6

Belief revision for temporary context

Tier C · medium
Previous research coverage

Dual-profile memory appends dictionary keys ({like, dislike}) linearly.

Insufficiently investigated boundary

Distinguishing temporary situational constraints (e.g. gift buying) from permanent taste shifts.

Bibliographic evidence

Freitas et al. (2026) · Meng et al. (2025)

Context

Appending to a profile is monotonic; a temporary need written into it never fully leaves, and the long-term profile absorbs the noise.

Proposed research question

What belief-revision algorithms can prevent temporary context queries from corrupting long-term user profiles?

The tiers imply an order of work rather than a ranking of difficulty. Tier A gaps are prerequisites: a joint seven-dimensional objective (G1) and a way to score unlogged items (G2) must exist before catalog-scale continual learning (G3) or governance-aware matching (G4) can be evaluated at all. Tier C gaps are enabling techniques that improve an architecture once it exists — differentiable prompt–neural spaces (G5) and belief revision (G6) — and each has a concrete, testable formulation rather than a general call for “more research”.

08/ 11

Threats to validity and limitations

تهدیدهای اعتبار و محدودیت‌ها

8.1 · Construct validity

The 7D framework is an interpretive instrument, not a measurement scale. Two coders could map the same study to different dimensions where a paper uses a mechanism for a purpose the authors do not name. Mitigation was procedural: each paper was extracted by one assigned member against the field definitions in §4.2, then audited by the group; disagreement was resolved by re-reading the method section, not by voting. The residual risk is that a dimension is credited because a mechanism could serve it.

8.2 · Internal validity

The corpus mixes evaluations that are not commensurable: NDCG on public benchmarks, bilateral recall in simulated markets, Hit@5 in a deployment, and compute-cost reductions. No effect size is pooled, and the synthesis compares representational choices, not performance. Claims such as “CL4SRec improves NDCG@10 by 7.37%–11.02%” are reported as the studies state them, against their own baselines.

8.3 · External validity

The corpus is weighted toward recommender systems and e-commerce, and two of the ten studies are industry deployments. Results transfer least well to domains where the item space is small and constraints are legal rather than commercial, such as public-service matching or clinical triage. The 2021–2026 window further excludes the pre-transformer period that founded much of the vocabulary used here.

8.4 · Conclusion validity

Tier assignments (A/B/C) are a team judgement about urgency and dependency, not a statistical result. A different weighting — for example treating governance as an immediate constraint rather than a Tier B concern — would reorder the roadmap while leaving the taxonomy intact.

8.5 · Review-level limitations

Four limitations belong to this review rather than to the corpus. The protocol was not registered; the corpus is purposive, being the assignment's benchmark set, so it cannot support a claim about the proportion of the literature containing a given approach; per-database screening counts were not archived, so Figure 1 reports stages rather than record counts; and single-coder extraction means the extraction has not been independently replicated. A full PRISMA flow diagram with counts would require re-running the searches with a logged protocol, which is the first item on the roadmap that concerns this review itself.

Conclusion and future work

نتیجه‌گیری و کارهای آینده

This review compared ten benchmark studies through a seven-dimensional account of what it means for a system to represent a changing person. Three findings are stable across the corpus. Sequential and temporal models (CL4SRec, TARN, SBRS) are mature in the two dimensions they target — history (D2) and drift (D7) — and are represented by nine of ten studies. LLM-based agents (InteRecAgent, Meng et al., Tan & Jiang) extend coverage into stated goals and constraints (D3–D5) but introduce latency and hallucination as first-order costs. Bilateral and lifelong work (CRRS, DITTO) addresses the two hardest structural problems — mutual satisfaction and continual adaptation — while explicitly assuming away parts of the user model it depends on.

The coverage map makes the imbalance concrete: the median study covers four of seven dimensions, and the two thinnest dimensions (D5 constraints, D6 environment) are also the two that decide whether a recommendation is permissible rather than merely relevant. The gap taxonomy converts that observation into six testable questions, ordered so that the two Tier A prerequisites come first.

Three directions follow directly. First, a single architecture that optimises the seven dimensions jointly, rather than chaining specialised components, is the precondition for the rest. Second, streaming cold-start scoring — admitting an item into the candidate set before it has behavioural evidence — is a concrete design problem with a measurable target. Third, making the language layer differentiable with respect to the ranking layer would remove the serialisation boundary that currently prevents joint training. Each is stated as a research question in Table 8 rather than as a recommendation, because none of the three can be settled with the current evidence.
